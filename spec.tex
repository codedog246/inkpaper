\section{Callout specimen}

Body text at 10pt for reference. Every callout shares one grammar: 0.45pt
rules, square corners, a 10pt inset, \verb|\small| text, and one label voice.
The four semantic boxes carry a solid title bar in a hue derived from the
palette accent, on a small lightness ladder (Takeaway darkest, Observation
lightest); the finding box is the quiet member of the family; the prompt box
is the neutral one.

\begin{takeaway}[label=spec:tk]
\textbf{Judge uncertainty identifies the rubric criteria most vulnerable to
reward hacking.} Most exploitation concentrates on a small set of criteria
where the judge was already uncertain before policy optimization.
\end{takeaway}

\begin{discussion}
\textbf{Relationship to rubric dropout.} Dropout makes a fixed rubric harder
to exploit by varying which criteria receive reward. Reliability weighting
addresses a complementary failure mode.
\end{discussion}

A paragraph of running text between two callouts, so the before and after
skips can be judged against ordinary paragraph rhythm, and so that
Takeaway~\ref{spec:tk} can be cross-referenced by number.

\begin{practicalguidance}
\textbf{Calibrate the judge before starting reinforcement learning.} Sample
each rubric criterion several times on representative prompts, estimate its
agreement, and downweight unstable criteria.
\end{practicalguidance}

\begin{observation}
\textbf{Reliability is a property of the criterion, not the prompt.}
Estimates vary little across prompts that share a criterion.
\end{observation}

\begin{finding}
Reliability weighting improves gold judge scores on every benchmark while
leaving in-domain rubric reward unchanged.
\end{finding}

\end{keybox}

\begin{promptbox}[Rubric judge prompt]
You are grading a response against a single rubric criterion.\\[0.4em]
Answer YES if the response satisfies the criterion, otherwise NO.
\end{promptbox}
